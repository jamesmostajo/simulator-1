Concepts

The ideas behind the simulator: the game, how agents predict, how they bet, and how results are measured.

The simulator asks a simple question: when the rules of a random system are hidden and keep shifting, how well can an agent predict it, and how should it bet on those predictions? Every agent sees the same coin flips. Each one combines a prediction strategy (how it estimates the next flip) with a betting strategy (how much of its stack to risk), and we compare how their stacks grow over time.

01Markov chains: the game

A Markov chain is a system that moves between a fixed set of states, where the chance of moving to each next state depends only on the current state, not on how it got there. It is described completely by a transition matrix: row i lists the probability of going from state i to every other state, and each row adds up to 1.

In this game the states are three coins. A fair coin lands tails 50% of the time, a heads-biased coin 25%, and a tails-biased coin 75%. After every flip the game may switch coins according to the matrix below. The game starts on the fair coin.

Two layers of hidden information

Agents only see the flips. They never see which coin is in play (the first hidden layer), and they are never told the coins' biases or how often the coins switch (the second hidden layer). This setup is called a hidden Markov model. A run of tails might mean the tails-biased coin is in play, or it might be luck on the fair coin, and by the time an agent is fairly sure, the coin may already have switched.

Why it is used here

A Markov chain is the simplest model of a system that is random but not structureless: there are patterns (coins persist for a few flips), yet they are hidden and keep changing. That makes it a clean test of whether agents can find structure in noise without being given the rules.

The numbers are deliberately hard. Coins stay for only about three flips, and the biases are modest, so even an agent that knew the rules perfectly could only predict about 55% of flips correctly.

02From probability to a signal

Every prediction strategy produces an estimate of P(tails), the chance the next flip is tails. The agent turns that into a signal between −1 and +1:

signal = 2 × P(tails) − 1
  • The sign picks the side: positive bets tails, negative bets heads.
  • The size is the confidence: 0 means "no idea" (50/50), 1 means "certain".

For example, P(tails) = 0.75 gives a signal of +0.5, a moderate lean towards tails.

Why it is used here

The signal separates prediction from betting. Every predictor speaks the same language, so any predictor can be paired with any betting strategy, and the simulator can test every combination. Its size is also exactly what Kelly betting needs (section 07).

03Window-based prediction

The window agent remembers only the last N flips and estimates P(tails) as the share of tails among them:

P(tails) = tails in the last N flips / N

A small window reacts quickly when the coin changes but is very noisy: a window of 2 can only say 0%, 50% or 100%. A large window is smooth but slow, still averaging over coins that are long gone.

Why it is used here

It is the most intuitive way to learn from recent data, and it assumes nothing about the game. The window sizes tested (2, 6, 10, 20, 50) range from shorter than a typical coin's stay to many times longer, so the results show the trade-off between reacting fast and averaging out noise.

04Exponential moving average (EMA)

The EMA agent keeps one running estimate and nudges it towards each new flip (1 for tails, 0 for heads):

P(tails) ← alpha × latest flip + (1 − alpha) × P(tails)

Instead of a hard cut-off, older flips fade away gradually: each flip's weight shrinks by a factor of (1 − alpha) every round. Alpha sets the memory, roughly the last 1 / alpha flips. A high alpha reacts fast and is noisy; a low alpha is smooth and slow. It starts at 0.5 (no opinion). The chart above shows it next to the window estimate.

Why it is used here

It captures the same idea as the window with a smoother memory, and only needs to store one number. The values tested (0.05, 0.2, 0.5) cover slow, medium and fast memory.

Both the window and the EMA can become overconfident. After a short streak they can report 90 to 100% confidence, while even an agent that knew the game's rules would never be more than about 58% sure. That matters a lot for Kelly betting.

05Tolerance

Tolerance is the minimum confidence needed to place a bet. If the size of the signal is not above the tolerance, the agent skips the round:

bet only if |signal| > tolerance

The figure shows the EMA signal from the chart above (using its alpha). Rounds inside the shaded band are skipped.

0.10
Why it is used here

Weak signals are mostly noise. Betting on them costs money without adding much edge, so a tolerance can filter them out. The values tested (0, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3) go from betting on everything to betting only on strong signals.

The trade-off is fewer bets. A tolerance that is too high can stop an agent from ever betting: a careful predictor whose signal never goes above 0.2 would sit out every round at tolerance 0.2.

06Fractional betting

A fractional bettor stakes the same share of its current stack every time it bets, whatever its confidence:

bet = size scale × stack

Because the bet is a share of the stack, wins and losses compound: bets grow after wins and shrink after losses, so the agent can never lose everything in one round. Only the direction of the prediction matters, so what counts is how often the agent is right. Why it is used here

It is the simplest sensible way to size bets, and a natural baseline. The sizes tested (1, 2, 5, 10 and 20% of the stack) range from very cautious to aggressive. It also isolates the predictor's direction from its confidence: two predictors that always pick the same side get identical results.

07Kelly betting and size scale

The Kelly criterion picks the share of the stack that makes it grow fastest over the long run. For an even-money bet won with probability p:

Kelly share = p − (1 − p) = 2p − 1 = |signal|

So with the signal from section 02, the Kelly bet is simply the signal's size as a share of the stack. A 60% belief bets 20%, and a 50% belief bets nothing. The figure shows the expected log growth per round for every share, given a true win chance p. Growth peaks at the Kelly share, falls on either side, and turns negative when betting about twice the Kelly share.

Size scale: betting a fraction of Kelly

Kelly is only optimal if the probability is right. The size scale multiplies the Kelly bet:

bet = size scale × |signal| × stack

Half Kelly (0.5) gives up about a quarter of the growth in exchange for much smaller swings, and protects against an overconfident estimate. Betting more than Kelly only adds risk.

Why it is used here

Kelly ties the bet to the agent's confidence, so it rewards calibrated predictions and punishes overconfident ones. That makes it the right tool for testing whether agents' probabilities are trustworthy, not just whether they pick the right side.

The sizes tested (0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0) run from quarter Kelly to full Kelly. Full Kelly is the textbook optimum only for an agent that knows the true probability, which only the oracle does. For agents that learn from flips, full Kelly tends to bet too much.

08Log stack and log growth

Each agent tracks the natural log of its stack instead of the stack itself. A bet that wins or loses a share f of the stack multiplies it by (1 + f) or (1 − f), and logs turn multiplying into adding:

log stack ← log stack + ln(1 + f) after a win log stack ← log stack + ln(1 − f) after a loss

Log growth is how far the log stack has moved from the start: ln(stack / starting stack). A log growth of 0 means even with the start, +1 means about 2.7 times the start, +2.3 means 10 times, and −0.69 means half. The stack itself is never needed.

Why averages of returns mislead

Win 50% and then lose 50%: the average return is 0%, but 100 chips become 150 and then 75. In logs this is ln(1.5) + ln(0.5) = −0.29, correctly negative. Repeated bets compound, so the log tells the true story of growth over time.

Why it is used here

Three reasons. Stacks grow exponentially (a successful agent's stack can pass 10300, where regular numbers overflow), and log values never overflow. Log growth is the quantity Kelly maximises, so it is the right measure of success. And on a log scale, steady compounding looks like a straight line, so agents with very different results can be compared on one chart.

09Minimum bet and bankruptcy

Every bet must be at least 1 chip. If a strategy asks for less (for example, 1% of an 80-chip stack is 0.8), the agent bets 1 instead. An agent that cannot cover 1 chip is bankrupt: it stops playing and its record ends at that round. A skipped round (signal under the tolerance) is different: the strategy chose not to bet, and the minimum does not apply.

In log terms, bankrupt means a log stack below ln(1) = 0, which is why a bankrupt agent's final log stack is negative.

Why it is used here

Without a minimum, a losing agent could keep betting tiny fractions forever and never really fail. The minimum makes ruin possible, as it is in a real game. Raising small bets to 1 chip (instead of skipping them) keeps cautious agents with small stacks playing, rather than leaving them frozen while they appear to survive.

10Baselines: random and oracle

Two agents frame every result. The random agent draws its P(tails) uniformly between 0 and 1 every round, so it has no skill. The oracle is told which coin produces each flip and predicts with the true probability, so it has the best possible information and bets full Kelly.

Why it is used here

A strategy only means something relative to these two bounds. Doing better than random shows real skill, not luck. The gap to the oracle shows how much the two hidden layers cost: no agent that sees only flips can reach the oracle, which is why it is shown separately on the results page.
